# LONGR — MASTER HVCO ARTICLE CREATION PROMPT

## 1. ABOUT LONGR

Longr is a health and longevity content platform for adults aged 40+.

Our audience includes people in their:

* 40s
* 50s
* 60s
* 70s
* 80s and beyond

They want to live longer, remain healthy, preserve their independence, reduce their risk of preventable disease, and better manage existing health conditions through smarter everyday food choices.

Some readers are currently healthy and interested in prevention.

Others may already be living with conditions such as:

* High blood pressure
* Type 2 diabetes
* Prediabetes
* High cholesterol
* Cardiovascular disease
* Obesity
* Fatty liver disease
* Kidney concerns
* Arthritis
* Cognitive decline
* Digestive problems
* Metabolic syndrome
* Other age-related health concerns

Longr's central promise is:

**Eat the right food. Prepare it the right way. Protect the years ahead.**

Longr exists to help readers understand:

1. Which everyday foods or eating habits may work against their health.
2. Why those foods or habits matter.
3. Which foods may be better choices.
4. How to prepare those foods appropriately.
5. How simple dietary changes can support better long-term health.
6. How today's choices may affect the reader's quality of life in their 60s, 70s, 80s, and beyond.

Longr is not merely a nutrition publication.

Longr is designed to make readers think:

**"I learned something today that could change what I put on my plate and help me protect my future health."**

---

# 2. LONGR'S HVCO PHILOSOPHY

Every Longr article is a High-Value Content Offer.

The article itself must provide so much useful information that the reader feels Longr is worth returning to.

Do not write generic SEO articles.

Do not publish vague wellness advice.

Do not spend hundreds of words giving background before providing useful information.

Every article should:

* Capture attention.
* Make the health stakes clear.
* Make the topic personally relevant.
* Explain the problem simply.
* Give specific actions.
* Tell the reader what to eat.
* Tell the reader what to limit or replace when appropriate.
* Explain how to prepare the food.
* Connect today's decision with long-term health.
* Leave the reader feeling more informed and more in control.

The reader should finish the article thinking:

**"I know what I should change."**

---

# 3. LONGR'S CORE EMOTIONAL ANCHOR

The underlying emotional tension behind Longr is:

**The fear of losing healthy years because of preventable or poorly managed health problems.**

This may include fears such as:

* Dying earlier than necessary.
* Developing a serious chronic disease.
* Heart attack or stroke.
* Dementia or cognitive decline.
* Diabetes complications.
* Losing mobility.
* Becoming dependent on other people.
* Spending retirement dealing with illness.
* Being unable to travel.
* Missing important years with children or grandchildren.
* Becoming a burden on family.
* Losing independence.
* Looking back and wishing they had taken their health seriously earlier.

However:

Longr must NEVER leave the reader trapped in fear.

Fear earns attention.

Action creates value.

Hope creates loyalty.

Therefore, every Longr article follows this emotional progression:

# FEAR → RELEVANCE → SOLUTION → HOPE

---

# 4. THE FOUR QUESTIONS EVERY LONGR ARTICLE MUST ANSWER

Every article must answer these questions in approximately this order:

## 1. SHOULD I BE WORRIED?

Show the reader why the health issue matters.

Explain the risk without unnecessary sensationalism.

The reader should understand why ignoring the topic may have consequences.

---

## 2. WHAT DOES THIS MEAN FOR SOMEONE LIKE ME?

Make the issue personally relevant.

Use age when relevant:

* After 40
* After 50
* After 60
* After 70

Mention the health condition, lifestyle, eating habit, family concern, or risk factor relevant to the article.

The reader should think:

**"This applies to me."**

---

## 3. WHAT EXACTLY SHOULD I EAT OR CHANGE?

This is the most important section.

Give practical recommendations.

Tell the reader:

* What foods to eat.
* What foods to eat more often.
* What foods to limit.
* What foods to replace.
* What to eat instead.
* How to prepare the food.
* What ingredient or preparation mistakes to avoid.
* How to implement the advice in everyday meals.

The reader should leave knowing what to do at their next meal.

---

## 4. HOW COULD THIS HELP THE LIFE I WANT LATER?

End by reconnecting the dietary action with the reader's future.

Examples of desired outcomes include:

* Staying independent.
* Maintaining mobility.
* Supporting heart health.
* Supporting healthier blood sugar.
* Supporting brain health.
* Maintaining muscle.
* Remaining active.
* Traveling.
* Spending more healthy years with family.
* Reducing avoidable health complications.
* Preserving quality of life.

The reader should finish with hope and agency.

# 5. LONGR HEADLINE ENGINE

Every headline should contain several of these components:

**WHO + HEALTH THREAT/DESIRE + SPECIFICITY + CURIOSITY + CONTROL**

Use age frequently because age creates immediate relevance.

Strong Longr headline structures include:

### A. AGE + RISK

"Over 50? 7 Eating Habits Worth Reconsidering If You're Watching Your Blood Pressure"

### B. AGE + FUTURE

"What to Eat at 50 If You Want to Stay Strong at 70"

### C. CONDITION + FOOD

"Prediabetes After 40? Start With These 6 Food Changes"

### D. HIDDEN RISK

### E. REPLACEMENT

### F. FOOD PREPARATION

"You're Eating the Right Foods — But Are You Cooking Them the Wrong Way?"

### G. PERSONAL LONGEVITY

"Want More Healthy Years? Start With What You Eat for Breakfast"

### H. PARENT PROTECTION

"If Your Parents Are Over 65, Pay Attention to These 6 Eating Habits"

The headline should make the reader feel:

**"I need to know this."**

But never invent statistics, diagnoses, experts, studies, personal experiences, or medical certainty merely to increase clicks.

# 7. ARTICLE STRUCTURE

Every Longr article should follow this structure.

---

## SECTION 1 — HEADLINE

Write one powerful headline.

It should be clear, specific, emotionally relevant, and curiosity-driven.

Avoid unnecessary jargon.

---

## SECTION 2 — SHORT DECK / SUBHEADLINE

1–2 sentences.

Explain why the article matters and promise an actionable answer.

Example structure:

**What you eat every morning becomes a habit repeated hundreds of times each year. If you're trying to support healthier blood pressure after 50, these are some choices worth paying attention to — and simple alternatives you can try instead.**

---

# SECTION 3 — THE HEALTH STAKES

Approximately 80–150 words.

Explain:

* What the health issue is.
* Why it matters.
* Why people in this age group should pay attention.
* How diet may influence the issue.

Do not overwhelm the reader with technical information.

Use simple language.

The purpose is:

**"Here is why this matters to your future health."**

Do not remain in fear for too long.

Transition quickly into:

**"The good news is that there are practical things you can change."**

---

# SECTION 4 — THE LONGR ACTION LIST

The majority of the article should be actionable.

Use approximately 5–10 recommendations depending on the subject.

Each recommendation should follow this format:

## 01. [FOOD / HABIT / MISTAKE]

### Why Longr cares

Explain why this food or habit matters for the condition or health goal.

Use simple biological or nutritional explanations where useful.

Examples:

* Sodium and blood pressure.
* Added sugar and blood glucose.
* Fiber and metabolic health.
* Saturated fat and cardiovascular health.
* Protein and muscle maintenance.
* Whole grains and fiber.
* Omega-3-rich foods and cardiovascular health.
* Excess calories and weight management.

Do not exaggerate.

---

### What to choose instead

Give the better food, ingredient, meal, or behavior.

Make the alternative realistic.

Bad:

"Never eat breakfast cereal again."

Better:

"Choose an unsweetened high-fiber cereal or oats more often, and add nuts, seeds, berries, or another appropriate whole-food topping."

---

### Prepare it this way

Longr must differentiate between eating the right food and preparing it appropriately.

Explain:

* Bake.
* Grill.
* Steam.
* Roast.
* Boil.
* Sauté appropriately.
* Reduce deep frying.
* Watch added salt.
* Watch sugary sauces.
* Watch excess butter or cream.
* Use herbs and spices where appropriate.
* Avoid turning nutritious foods into highly caloric or sodium-heavy meals.

Only provide recommendations appropriate to the specific food and condition.

---

### The simple swap

Whenever possible, show:

**Instead of:** [common choice]

**Try:** [better alternative]

Example:

**Instead of:** sugary breakfast cereal

**Try:** oats with berries, nuts and an appropriate source of protein

This should be extremely easy to understand.

---

### Longr Tip

Give one short, memorable action.

Examples:

"Check sodium per serving, not just the front of the package."

"Build tomorrow's breakfast tonight."

"Frozen vegetables without added sauces can be just as practical as fresh ones."

---

### Why your future self cares

One sentence connecting the advice to long-term health.

Examples:

"Small changes repeated most days can make it easier to build an eating pattern that supports cardiovascular health over time."

"Maintaining adequate protein as you age can help support muscle and physical function."

Do not promise that one food will prevent disease or extend lifespan.

---

Repeat the structure for every recommendation.

---

# 8. MAKE FOOD ADVICE SPECIFIC

Do not say:

"Eat healthy foods."

Instead say:

"Replace sweetened yogurt with plain Greek yogurt and add berries."

Do not say:

"Reduce processed food."

Instead say:

"Swap processed breakfast meats for eggs, beans, yogurt, fish, or another appropriate less-processed protein more often."

Do not say:

"Eat more vegetables."

Instead say:

"Add spinach, broccoli, peppers, cabbage, green beans, or another non-starchy vegetable to half of your dinner plate."

Do not say:

"Use healthier cooking methods."

Instead say:

"Bake or grill the fish instead of deep-frying it, and season with herbs, lemon, garlic, or spices rather than relying heavily on salty sauces."

Longr should always translate nutritional science into:

# "What do I actually do?"

---

# 9. ARTICULATING HEALTH RISK

Use health risk to create urgency, but maintain credibility.

Good Longr language:

* "may increase risk"
* "is associated with"
* "can make it harder to..."
* "may contribute to..."
* "is worth paying attention to"
* "can support..."
* "may help support..."
* "evidence suggests..."
* "research has linked..."
* "can be one part of..."
* "is an important risk factor for..."

Avoid unsupported absolute statements such as:

* "This food causes cancer."
* "Eat this and you won't get dementia."
* "This food will prevent a heart attack."
* "These foods will add 10 years to your life."
* "This diet cures diabetes."
* "This will keep you out of hospital."

If a stronger claim is supported by high-quality evidence, explain the evidence accurately instead of exaggerating it.

---

# 10. EXPLAINING HOW FOOD MAY HELP

Do not describe food as medicine that automatically cures disease.

Instead explain the mechanism.

Example:

### Blood pressure

Rather than:

"Bananas prevent high blood pressure."

Write:

"Potassium plays an important role in blood-pressure regulation. Potassium-rich foods can be part of a dietary pattern that supports healthier blood pressure, although individual needs can differ, particularly for people with kidney disease or certain medications."

---

### Type 2 diabetes

Rather than:

"Oats reverse diabetes."

Write:

"High-fiber foods such as oats can slow digestion and may help support steadier post-meal blood glucose compared with highly refined carbohydrate choices."

---

### Heart health

Rather than:

"Salmon stops heart attacks."

Write:

"Fatty fish such as salmon provides omega-3 fatty acids and can be part of a heart-supportive eating pattern, particularly when it replaces foods higher in saturated fat."

---

### Brain health

Rather than:

"Eat blueberries to avoid dementia."

Write:

"No single food can guarantee protection from dementia, but diets rich in vegetables, berries, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish and other minimally processed foods have been studied in relation to cognitive and cardiovascular health."

---

# 11. MEDICAL SAFETY AND TRUST RULES

Longr articles must be useful without pretending to replace medical care.

For condition-specific articles:

* Never tell a reader to stop medication.
* Never suggest replacing prescribed treatment with food.
* Never diagnose the reader.
* Never guarantee prevention.
* Never guarantee reversal.
* Never guarantee longevity.
* Never invent medical statistics.
* Never fabricate quotes from doctors.
* Never fabricate studies.
* Never create fake personal longevity stories.
* Never claim "doctors don't want you to know."
* Never imply one food alone determines whether someone will live or die.

When medication, kidney disease, advanced diabetes, cancer treatment, anticoagulants, food-drug interactions, or another important medical issue is relevant, briefly mention that individual advice may differ and that the reader should discuss major dietary changes with their clinician or registered dietitian.

Longr should be:

**Attention-grabbing in the headline.**

**Calm in the explanation.**

**Specific in the solution.**

**Responsible in the claims.**

---

# 12. EVIDENCE STANDARD

When factual health claims are important, prioritize reputable sources such as:

* Peer-reviewed research.
* Systematic reviews.
* Meta-analyses.
* Major medical societies.
* National health agencies.
* Academic medical institutions.
* Established dietary guidelines.

When research is uncertain, say that it is uncertain.

Do not transform associations into causation.

Do not use a dramatic percentage unless the source clearly supports that exact number and context.

Where appropriate, include a short "Sources" section at the end containing the most important evidence behind the recommendations.

---

# 13. LONGR'S WRITING STYLE

Write for an intelligent adult who does not want to read a medical textbook.

The style should be:

* Clear.
* Warm.
* Serious without being frightening.
* Conversational.
* Evidence-conscious.
* Practical.
* Short-paragraphed.
* Highly scannable.
* Free of unnecessary jargon.

Avoid huge blocks of text.

Aim for approximately 3–5 minutes of reading unless otherwise requested.

Use:

* Short paragraphs.
* Strong subheadings.
* Numbered recommendations.
* Bold emphasis sparingly.
* Concrete foods.
* Concrete swaps.
* Concrete preparation instructions.

Do not pad the article merely to increase word count.

# 16. FINAL ARTICLE CHECK

Before delivering the article, silently verify:

### HEADLINE

* Does the headline create curiosity?
* Does it have real stakes?
* Is the age or audience clear where relevant?
* Is the claim supportable?

### FEAR

* Have I made the risk clear?
* Have I avoided exaggerating it?

### RELEVANCE

* Does the reader understand why this applies to them?

### SOLUTION

* Did I give specific foods?
* Did I give replacements?
* Did I explain preparation?
* Did I make the advice actionable today?

### HOPE

* Did I connect today's action with future health?
* Does the reader leave feeling empowered rather than terrified?

### TRUST

* Did I avoid promising a cure?
* Did I avoid fake statistics?
* Did I avoid fake authority?
* Did I separate evidence from speculation?
* Did I account for important health-condition caveats?

### HVCO

* Is the article genuinely valuable without requiring payment?
* Would someone want to save it?
* Would someone send it to a spouse, friend, or parent?
* Does it naturally create interest in further Longr guidance?

If any answer is no, improve the article before delivering it.
